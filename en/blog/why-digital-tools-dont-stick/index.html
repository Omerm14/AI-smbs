Why the last digital tool you tried didn't stick — and what actually does

By Omer Moran, Founder & Principal, AAA · Published July 16, 2026

Off-the-shelf tools demand that the business bend around them, which is why they get abandoned. The fix is working the other way: understand how your business already runs, then build a system that fits over the existing habits — nobody has to change how they work, so there's nothing to "stick."

The pattern, if you've lived it

It usually goes the same way. A tool gets purchased because it solves a real problem on paper. Someone runs a training session. For a week or two, the team uses it alongside the old method, "just to be safe." Then a busy day hits, the old WhatsApp thread or spreadsheet is faster because it's already the path of least resistance, and the new tool quietly becomes the thing that gets updated once a week instead of the thing the business actually runs on. Within a couple of months, it's effectively shelfware — not because the tool was bad, but because it asked people to change a habit that was working well enough to not be worth the switching cost.

What "fits the business" means in practice

The alternative isn't a better interface — it's not requiring a new one at all where possible. Concretely:

  • Floory reads orders wherever the bakery's clients already send them — portal or plain WhatsApp — instead of requiring every client to adopt a new ordering app.
  • Cashflow reads supplier invoices from Gmail, Drive, and WhatsApp exactly as they already arrive, instead of requiring a new upload portal.
  • The WhatsApp agent lives inside the same WhatsApp thread a customer already uses, instead of routing them to a separate chat widget.

In every case, the team's day-to-day habits stay the same. What changes is what happens automatically in the background.

A quick self-check before you buy the next tool

  1. Does it require your team or your customers to move off a channel they already use daily (WhatsApp, email, a spreadsheet)?
  2. Does "using it properly" depend on someone remembering to log in and update it, rather than it working passively in the background?
  3. If the person who championed it left tomorrow, would the rest of the team keep using it without being told to?

If the honest answer to any of these is "that's a real risk," it's worth asking whether the system can be built to sit on top of what already works instead.
